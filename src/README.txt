The Hierarchy of Sentence Processing Levels in L1 and L2: A Stage-1 Registered Report

There have been findings to support the notion that hierarchically shallow syntactic structures and verbatim word forms are overrepresented in the mental text model of recently processed L2 input. Occasionally, this has taken the form of an L2 advantage in the performance of tasks sensitive to such surface-level information. Compared to native speakers, non-native speakers have shown greater sensitivity to the alternation of semantically equivalent lexical and syntactic structures when reading multiple versions of texts (Bordag et al., 2021), have indicated better verbatim retention of non-standard lexical items in sentence recall (Sampaio & Konopka, 2013), and have been less likely to form false memories for critical items when presented with lists of related words using the Deese-Roediger-McDermott (DRM) paradigm (Arndt & Beato, 2017; Gor et al., 2021; Suarez & Beato, 2023). Explanations for these findings have appealed first to the shallow structure hypothesis (SSH), which posits qualitatively distinct processing strategies between L1 and L2 defined by structural dimensions (Clahsen & Felser, 2018). Reyna’s fuzzy trace theory (FTT) analogously dichotomizes the representation of recalled information into amorphous “gist” and eidetic, surface-level memories (Reyna, 2012, p. 333). Gist-level memories support sophisticated, intuition-based inference that, in the developmental context, is associated with intellectual maturity, whereas the surface level, although associated with greater precision, correlates negatively with reasoning quality. The SSH and the FTT are compatible with a range of findings that reveal qualitatively distinct processing strategies in L1 and L2 defined by a perceived gradient of depth or sophistication, such as the analytic and sublexical routes associated with L2, contrasting more holistic processing in L1, that are negatively correlated with proficiency (Jiang et al., 2020), or the weaker response to subtext in L2 moral judgements (Costa et al., 2014). The vocabulary of the FTT has been repurposed to some extent in the fuzzy lexical representation (FLR) hypothesis, which uses “fuzzy” to denote the weak form-meaning connections in the L2 lexicon rather than the gist-based nature of the L1 lexicon (Gor et al., 2021, p. 2). The FLRH accounts for, for example, the findings that free alternation between synonyms is inhibited in the output as a function of L2-group status and decreasing proficiency (Liu & Zhong, 2016), and conversely, that L2 users are more sensitive to synonymous substitutions in the input (Sampaio & Konopka, 2013). Despite their contrasting applications of the adjective “fuzzy,” the two theories overlap on the notion that superficial mental representations of text such as those in L2 are poor in underlying semantic content and therefore resist consolidation with other lexical traces, which would rely upon semantic mediation, resulting in greater L2 surface-level precision in certain tasks (Gor et al., 2021).
The thread of a representational dichotomy in the mental structure of human language stretches back to the beginning of the modern field of linguistics (Saussure, 1916; Chomsky, 1957; Chomsky, 1965). Extensive empirical research into language processing in the field of SLA (second language acquisition) has maintained a set of theories that continue to uphold multiple distinct levels of representation. I argue that what emerges from these findings is not, in fact, a simple dichotomy, but a representational hierarchy, whereby the L2 mental text model of processed input is characterized by the following order of priority: first by verbatim lexical retention (SSH, FTT, FLRH), then linear syntactic organization (SSH), thirdly by hierarchical syntactic organization (SSH), and, finally, by semantic trace (SSH, FTT, FLRH). It is anticipated that the distance in quantity between the proportion of the L2 mental text model devoted to the verbatim word form and that which is devoted to the semantic content will be the greatest, with there being a comparatively smaller quantitative distance between reserves of linear and hierarchical syntactic information. More generally, this organization can be represented as lexis > syntax > semantics, with linearity and hierarchy forming the two dimensions of syntax. It is anticipated that the L2 mental text model will differ systematically from the L1 mental text model in reverse hierarchical order: the greatest difference will be in semantic content, followed by syntactic information, and, finally, verbatim lexical information. It is expected that with proficiency, L2 speakers will approach nativelikeness at a rate corresponding to this same reverse hierarchical order; that is, as proficiency increases for an L2 speaker, gains in the proportion of their mental text model devoted to semantic content will be the greatest whereas gains in word form retention will be the smallest.
Measuring the mental text model holistically is a tricky matter. The knowledge that different types of linguistic features may activate shallower and deeper levels of representation differently between L1 and L2 to an unknown extent (Opitz et al., 2024) frustrates the prospect of applying traditional psycholinguistic techniques, which must target specific linguistic manipulations. Therefore, the present study implements a novel method based on natural language processing (NLP) techniques and quantitative linguistic theory to extract purely syntactic and purely semantic representations from the L1 and L2 output of an elicited imitation task with the goal of abstracting the mental levels of representation that generated the output. It is expected that algorithms operationalizing the lexical, linear-syntactic, hierarchical-syntactic, and semantic levels of representation will be able to extrapolate the proportions of the mental text model in each participant on the basis of what is retained from the stimulus. Therefore, the research questions are as follows:
RQ1: To what extent does the L1 vs. L2 difference in retention of linguistic information in an elicited imitation task, as measured via multidimensional computational analysis, correspond to the theoretically predicted hierarchical levels of representation?
RQ2: To what extent does increasing L2 proficiency correspond to increased nativelikeness according to the same representational hierarchy?
There will be evidence in support of question 1 if the effect sizes of L2 group status on each of the four measures of levels of representation correspond to the expected reverse hierarchical order. There will be evidence in support of question 2 if the effect sizes of proficiency on each of the four measures correspond to the same order.
Literature Review
Theoretical Background in Linguistics
Chomsky’s seminal works on syntax (1957, 1965) first popularized the concept of a representational dichotomy in human language, but the notion of surface and deep levels of representation can be traced back further, to Saussure’s “langue” and “parole” (1916, p. 6), through Wittgenstein’s “depth grammar” and “surface grammar” (McGinn, 2011, p. 646), and Hockett’s “deep grammar” and “surface grammar” (1958, p. 246). From the beginning of the so-called “Linguistics Wars,” there was a contention from generative semanticists that deep structure is semantic first, and that the underlying meaning drives the syntactic representation, not the other way around (Harris, 1995). Chomsky adhered to syntactic transformation between deep structure and surface structure as two syntactically defined levels, a scheme logically mandated by the existence of the learnability problem (1965). Current theories in SLA and psycholinguistics enjoy the benefit of being subject to rigorous empirical testing, which was never a part of Chomsky’s program. The programs that have emerged from this continual refinement in the domain of applied linguistics include elements of both the syntactician’s and the semanticist’s perspectives, but push a frontier far beyond their intellectual antecedents, to a more comprehensive and more nuanced understanding of the mental structure of language than was achievable through a purely theoretical paradigm.
Representational Dichotomies in SLA
The theoretical position in this study is built upon the synthesis in Bordag et al. (2021), who proposed that future research should investigate the differences in the L1 and L2 mental text models in terms of a tradeoff between the construction of abstract syntactic structures and retention of surface information. Such research could shed more light on the qualitative distinction between L1 and L2 sentence processing and quantify some of the applicable constructs of the SSH, the FLRH, and the FTT. At the heart of these theories are analogously dichotomized levels of representation: for the FTT and the FLRH, they are gist and superficial representations, mapping approximately to semantic content and form, whereas for the SSH, they are shallow and hierarchical dimensions of syntactic structure. A map of various representational dichotomies in theories of SLA and linguistics is presented in Figure 1. The parallel dichotomies of the FTT and the SSH have been investigated in various forms, in research targeting specific structures, using the DRM paradigm (Sıtkı et al., 2024), reaction times (Lin et al., 2024), fMRI (Pliatsikas et al., 2017), and eye tracking (Witzel et al., 2011; Schlenter, 2019) as measures of their contrasting corollaries in language processing.

Nevertheless, Bordag’s (2021) novel reorganization and synthesis of these theoretical trajectories around the principle of a qualitative difference in the construction of the L2 mental text model demands consideration of what these dichotomies mean in terms of language use. L2 speakers seem to prioritize verbatim information both as a developmental adaptation (according to usage-based theories) and as a way to compensate for deficits in hierarchical syntactic processing (according to the SSH), both in terms of lexis (supported by the FTT and the FLRH) and syntax (SSH). Therefore, the component structure of the L2 mental text model should consist primarily in lexical items (types) and linear (as opposed to hierarchical) syntactic information, but the model’s composition should be relatively poor in semantic content and hierarchical syntactic information. A visualization of this hierarchy in relation to that of the L1 mental text model is displayed in Figure 2.
Figure 2
The Representational Hierarchy of the L2 Mental Text Model
 
The L1 mental text model most likely prioritizes semantic content—or gist-based representations—and hierarchical syntactic relations, while suppressing verbatim lexical and linear syntactic organization. In this figure, linear syntax and verbatim lexical (type) levels of representation are grouped together as “surface,” whereas hierarchical syntax and semantic representation are grouped together as “gist.” The arrows indicate that the surface only imperfectly encodes the gist, whereas the gist readily and almost completely encodes the surface form. This is why the L1 mental text model is able to suppress the surface levels with no loss in fidelity from the semantic level, hence the directionality of this “hierarchy.” Linear syntax and hierarchical syntax are measurement-based conceptualizations applied to hypothesized underlying phenomena, whereas semantics is a theoretical linguistic construct standing in place of the cognitive construct of meaning, which exists outside of language. In this paper, therefore, the following shorthand is also used to refer to the hypothesized underlying constructs corresponding to the levels: “surface-lexis,” “surface-syntax,” “deep-syntax,” and “deep-meaning.”
The L2 Growth Trajectory
The current study takes a cue from the future directions proposed in Bordag et al. (2021), which take the form of specific research recommendations, on the one hand, and a more open call for new methodologies to expand upon and refine their novel hypothesis. A theoretical connection to be explored further is the role of verbatim retention in language development. According to usage-based theories, grammatical rules are abstracted from memorized sequences or “chunks” in the course of second language development (Bordag et al., p. 599). The characteristic emphasis on verbatim memorization in L2—and consequently the horizontal expansion of the L2 lexicon that prioritizes accumulation of tokens at the expense of semantic depth or consolidation of types—could be a unique adaptation of the adult interlanguage that facilitates the acquisition process. Research investigating this question should cover a range of proficiency levels in order to effectively identify growth trajectories.
A Holistic Measure of Processing Differences
The question of the “actual length of verbatim retention” raised in Bordag et al. (2021, p. 609) practically demands thinking about the mental text model in holistic terms. Nevertheless, there is an absence of methods that can directly measure what is retained and what is lost between the input and output stages, the space where the mental text model is realized and linguistic form presented in the input is reconstructed for output. In the present study, an elicited imitation task (EIT) is chosen to serve as the closest possible approximation of the mental text model in reconstructed, externalized form. In this task, participants are presented with a series of utterances, one by one, that they are required to reproduce immediately. Commonly used as a measure of proficiency, EIT has been convincingly upheld as an index of implicit linguistic knowledge, (Erlam & Wei, 2024) with very minimal sensitivity to short-term working memory capacity (Y. Kim et al., 2016); instead, it relies upon the participant’s ability to process the stimulus and reconstruct it in real time (Park et al., 2020). On a basic level, an output task like EIT provides an answer to the question of what is retained from a sequence of language presented as input. Being the only such task that also taps into implicit knowledge, it is ideally positioned to serve the purpose of inferring the properties of the internalized text model in multidimensional terms; in a sense, reverse engineering the language processing strategy that led from input to output. To do so, however, will require a novel methodological framework that might complicate assumptions about what topics can be explored through linguistic “products” in the study of second language development (Leow, 2015, p. 47).
Computational EIT Scoring
Increasingly sophisticated methods have been developed for scoring EITs, some employing computational methods (Culbertson et al., 2020; Isbell et al., 2023) and natural language processing (NLP) technology (Löber et al., 2024; K. M. Kim et al., 2024). So far, however, the primary goal of these efforts has been to streamline or automate the scoring of EITs and to improve their functioning as proficiency tests. To date, only a few studies have used an EIT-based protocol to investigate differences between L1 and L2 processing. Sampaio et al.’s recall task does not properly qualify as an EIT because it uses batch rather than sequential recall (2013). Nekrasova (2009) investigated verbatim recall of lexical bundles in L2 using a pseudo-EIT with findings that align with Bordag et al.’s hypothesis, namely that L2 participants recalled more bundles verbatim than the L1 participants did. Nevertheless, there are methodological limitations to her study including a low sample size, a small and poorly defined set of test items (Jeong & Jiang, 2019), and several departures from canonical EIT protocols that are thought to reinforce the sensitivity of the task to implicit knowledge (Erlam, 2006; Ortega, 2002).
Implicit Knowledge
The advantage of an EIT for the present study is that it is a measure of implicit knowledge that also provides analyzable output. That being said, the notion of implicit knowledge, to the extent that it pertains to the present study design, requires some unpacking. Generally, implicit knowledge entails a lack of awareness compared to explicit knowledge, which is the type of knowledge that is characterized by memorization and deployed through consciously access to stores of linguistic information. Generally, the former is associated with mastery and L1 status, whereas the latter is associated with low proficiency and L2 status. The declarative procedural (DP) model is invoked in Bordag et al. (2021) to explain that the L2 prioritization of verbatim or superficial information might be mandated by a deficiency in implicit, or procedural knowledge that is made up for by the activation of declarative, or explicit knowledge. Therefore, verbatim or surface-level information—which explicit knowledge integrates more readily than it does information deeper down the hierarchy—is disproportionately recruited in L2 in tasks that, in L1, would primarily draw upon implicit knowledge. The careful reader will note here that, in Bordag et al. (2021), yet another major theoretical dichotomy is reidentified with the underlying phenomenon of stratified levels of representation in the L1 and L2 mental text models. If the EIT indexed implicit knowledge alone, it might suffer poor validity in the present implementation because of the possibility of a qualitative difference between what a procedural/implicit knowledge model extracts from a text in L1 and what a declarative/explicit one does in L2. Nevertheless, on the occasions when the EIT’s status as a measure of implicit knowledge has been challenged, a more nuanced picture has come to light. It is possible that, in a proficiency-dependent manner, the EIT assesses not only implicit knowledge, but also automatized explicit knowledge, with the latter being associated with L2 performance in these tasks (Suzuki & DeKeyser, 2015). For these reasons, there is some assurance of the suitability of the EIT for investigating the topic at hand. Whether implicit or explicit—procedural or declarative—there is evidence that both types of knowledge are captured by EITs.
The Proposed Study
The output from an EIT can be computationally analyzed for its distance from the corresponding input. This is how EIT scoring works in general. The proposed study, however, will take a multidimensional approach—the semantic, syntactic (across a range of measures), and lexical distance between the input and the output will be assessed, providing a source of data that can be used to measure the processing of L1 and L2 speakers of varying proficiency levels across the multiple dimensions of text and to quantify the representational hierarchy spanning the surface and gist levels. The linguistic analysis will be accomplished using computational methods including dependency tree polynomial distance quantification (DTPD), modified morpheme error rate (MMER), and machine-learning-driven semantic textual similarity (STS) modeling. The goal of the proposed study is to contribute to the conversation on the qualitative difference between the L1 and L2 mental text models by investigating the extent to which computational measures of lexical, syntactic, and semantic similarity in EIT output align with theoretically organized gist-based and surface levels of representation in L1 and L2 language processing and how language status (L1/L2) and L2 proficiency level predict differential reliance on these hierarchical levels of representation.
Method
Design
Participants
For the pilot study, Isbell et al.’s (2023) elicited imitation dataset was subjected to the same analysis that will be employed in the full study. The participants were 198 L2 Korean speakers, mostly L1 Chinese female university students of late AO. There were 6 L1 Korean speakers presented as a control group. Since Isbell et al.’s study was oriented towards proficiency scoring rather than making comparisons between L1 and L2 processing, there was no need to have a high number of L1 speakers.
Following the results of the pilot study, a power analysis was run for the purposes of establishing the full study’s sample. Based on a minimum of 80% statistical power, the final study will plan to have a minimum of 25 L1 participants and 40 L2 participants. Details of the analysis conducted are included in the results section. The participants for the full study will be recruited from a local community organization. All will be L2 speakers of English with Korean as their L1. The age range will be 18-40 with a mix of AOs and proficiency levels.
The Task Procedure
The study (Isbell et al., 2023) that produced the data used in the pilot analysis used Y. Kim et al.’s (2016) Korean version of Ortega et al.’s (2002) standard English elicited imitation task. The 30 items are presented in increasing length from 7 to 19 syllables. Length is understood to be predictive of item difficulty, but an absence of other clear controls in the construction of the items is a weakness of these EIT materials from the standpoint of assessing language processing differences.
The full study, however, will use materials constructed based on a critical syntactic alternation known to increase processing difficulty—center embedding. Based on Gibson’s syntactic prediction locality theory, it is expected that increasing center-embeddedness will amplify processing burdens both for L1 and L2 (1998). This increase in difficulty will prevent ceiling effects and prohibit the language processor from defaulting to memorization or overt transfer of forms (Erlam & Wei, 2024). Because the language processor will be forced to economize with the increasing cognitive burden, L1 and L2 processing differences will be cast in increasingly high relief from control items, to singly center-embedded items, to doubly center-embedded items. The materials will consist of 50 sentences total:
•	10 control (relative clause, no center-embedding)
•	20 single center-embedded
•	20 lexically-matched double, nested center-embedded
All sentences will be matched for
•	Length (14-16 words)
•	Lexical frequency
•	Syntactic complexity (excluding embedding)
An example of matched pairs with one and two center-embedded clauses is provided below, with a “#” indicating the implausible double-embedded sentence:
•	The researcher who the university hired had impressed the professor who designed the experiment.
•	The professor who the researcher who the university hired had impressed designed the experiment.
The entire set of materials is included in the Appendix. Following the examples in Gibson (1998), the single- and double-embedded sentences contain exactly the same lexical items, just rearranged. A randomized list of 10 non-repeating single and double-embedded sentences from the 20 total in each category will be presented to each participant. Therefore, there will only be 30 items in the task—the same 10 control items for every participant, and a randomized 10 of each target item—single- and double-embedded sentence items—ensuring that a single-embedded item does not co-occur with its match for the same participant. Each participant’s task items will be presented in randomized order.
Participants will be administered a basic demographic questionnaire including background information such as languages spoken, AO, and length of time spent in an English-speaking country. Proficiency will be assessed via administration of Ortega et al.’s (2002) elicited imitation task. This task is chosen for two reasons: first, it has a long history of validation and use in SLA as a measure of L2 proficiency (Y. Kim et al, 2016); second, it will serve as a primer to the study task, which follows a similar format. Scoring will be completed with K. M. Kim et al.’s (2024) automated method using commercial NLP support, eliminating the need for human raters. All four models will be run, and both individual split-half and inter-rater reliability tests will be conducted upon their scores. The final proficiency scores will be composite scores weighted according to the split-half reliability results.
Proficiency test and task items will both be presented auditorily at a controlled presentation rate of 150 words per minute using a high-quality recording. Participants will be prompted for immediate verbal recall. There will be a short break every 10 minutes, and the total task duration will be about 30 minutes. Responses will be transcribed using automated speech recognition (ASR) with Naver’s RESTful API within Java’s HTTP client as in Isbell et al. (2023) and checked against the recordings by a human researcher.
Pilot Data Elicitation Procedure
Response data from Isbell et al.’s (2023) elicited imitation study was organized into an excel sheet where rows represent items and columns represent participants. A novel computational sentence processing level comparison, structured as pairwise comparisons between each item text and the corresponding output text for all responses in the sheet, was conducted using a pair of scripts written in Python for the purpose of this study. The primary packages used for this work include Stanza (Qi et al., 2020) for dependency parsing, KoNLPy (Park & Cho, 2014) for Korean-specific morphological analysis, and various numerical processing libraries including NumPy (Harris et al., 2020). Additionally, the semantic analysis script employs the KR-SBERT-V40K-klueNLI-augSTS model (Park & Shin, 2021), a Korean-specific adaptation of the Sentence-BERT architecture, which is popular in commercial applications (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019). GitHub Copilot was employed for code completions within Visual Studio Code. Both scripts are included in the Supplementary Materials. The measures included in each script are as follows:
•	Script 1 
o	Modified morpheme error rate, or MMER (operationalizing surface-lexis)
o	Dependency tree polynomial distance, or DTPD (operationalizing syntax)
o	Linear syntactic distance, or LSD (operationalizing surface-syntax)
o	Hierarchical syntactic distance, or HSD (operationalizing deep-syntax)

•	Script 2 
o	Semantic textual similarity, or STS (operationalizing deep-meaning)
STS was separated into its own script to facilitate debugging, since the other four measures can be executed relatively quickly and cheaply. The syntactic measures are all based on dependency grammar (Tesnière, 1959) and draw upon recent innovations in computational linguistics. First among the syntactic measures is a polynomial representation system that encodes syntactic relationships through dependency trees. It is my own implementation of Liu et al. (2022) built on the dependency parsing framework of Stanza (Qi et al., 2020). In this system, each type of dependency relationship is represented by a unique variable, with the complete set encompassing 37 distinct dependency types (e.g., adverbial clause modifier, nominal subject, direct object) from the Universal Dependencies framework (Nivre et al., 2021). For a given sentence, the script constructs a polynomial where each term represents a specific dependency relationship, and its coefficient indicates the frequency of that relationship. Specifically, it employs two sets of variables: xᵢ (i = 1 to 37) for standard dependency relationships and yᵢ (i = 1 to 37) for root-node relationships. This distinction allows it to capture both hierarchical relationships within the sentence and the unique properties of root elements. A visual representation of the tree distinguishing polynomial of an example sentence from Liu et al. (2022, p. 6) is included in Figure 3. It can be seen that each node is defined recursively upwards toward the root node, which consequently is labeled with the completed tree-distinguishing polynomial. The tree-distinguishing polynomial captures all of the syntactic information in a sentence, abstracting it away from all semantic content.


Figure 3
The Tree-Distinguishing Polynomial 
 
The distance between two sentences' syntactic structures is quantified through what I term the Dependency Tree Polynomial Distance (DTPD), or the Manhattan distance between the vectors of the tree-distinguishing polynomials of each sentence. Given two sentences S₁ and S₂, their respective polynomials are first converted to 74-dimensional vectors (v₁ and v₂), where each dimension corresponds to either an x-term or y-term coefficient. The DTPD is then calculated as:
Formula 1
DTPD = Σ|v₁ᵢ - v₂ᵢ| for i = 1 to 74
where v₁ᵢ and v₂ᵢ represent the i-th components of the respective vectors. DTPD can be considered a more inclusive measure than the dimensional hierarchical and linear measures that follow. Applied to the present task, it operationalizes the proportion of the total syntactic information retained from the stimulus.
To capture the hierarchical properties (HSD) of sentence structure, drawing on Sohn’s theoretical work in Korean syntax (2001), the script defines a set of metrics that evaluate different aspects of syntactic organization. The first of these is tree depth (D), calculated recursively for each token t in the sentence:
Formula 2
D(t) = max({D(c) | c ∈ children(t)}) + 1
where children(t) represents the set of tokens directly dependent on t.
Complementing this, it calculates the branching ratio (B) as the mean number of dependents per word:
Formula 3
B = (1/n)Σ|children(wᵢ)| for i = 1 to n
where n is the number of words in the sentence and |children(wᵢ)| denotes the number of dependents for word wᵢ.
To capture non-local dependencies, it introduces a long-distance dependency measure (L):
Formula 4
L = |{w | |pos(w) - pos(head(w))| > 3}|
where pos(w) denotes the position of word w in the sentence, and head(w) represents its syntactic head.
Finally, it incorporates a topic-structure depth measure (T) specific to Korean:
Formula 5
T = max({|children(w)| | w ∈ topic_marked_words})
These four metrics are combined into a hierarchical feature vector h = [D, B, L, T]. The hierarchical distance between two sentences is then computed as the L1 norm of their feature vector difference. These metrics are implemented using Stanza’s dependency parsing output, with specific adaptations for Korean topic-comment structure.
My analysis of linear structure (LSD) is based on mean dependency distance, or MDD (Chen et al., 2022) but focuses also on local word order patterns and particle placement, which are particularly relevant for Korean's relatively flexible word order (Lee & Ramsey, 2000). The script defines three primary metrics for linear structure analysis. The first is a transition probability measure (P) that captures local word order patterns:
Formula 6
P = |unique_transitions|/N
where N is the total number of transitions in the sentence, and a transition is defined as an adjacent pair of part-of-speech tags.
For particle analysis, it introduces a particle position metric (π):
Formula 7
π = (1/m)Σ(pos(pᵢ)/n) for i = 1 to m
where m is the number of particles in the sentence, pos(pᵢ) is the position of particle i, and n is the sentence length.
The third component is the mean dependency length (μ):
Formula 8
μ = (1/k)Σ|pos(w) - pos(head(w))| for all words w with heads
where k is the number of words with heads in the sentence.
These metrics are computed using a combination of Stanza’s dependency parsing and KoNLPy’s morphological analysis capabilities. 
My implementation of MMER is a modified version of the traditional word error rate (WER) metric (Morris et al., 2004), specifically adapted for Korean morphological structure. Unlike standard WER calculations that consider insertions, deletions, and substitutions equally, my retention-focused approach modifies the Levenshtein distance algorithm to emphasize retained information. The calculation is performed at the morpheme level using KoNLPy’s Okt analyzer and is formalized as:
Formula 9
MMER = (S + I) / max(|R|, |H|)
where S represents substitutions and I represents insertions, |R| is the length of the reference sequence, and |H| is the length of the hypothesis sequence. Notably, deletion errors are not penalized, since the purpose of the study is to quantify what is retained in the mental text model, not what is lost. 
For semantic analysis, I employ the KR-SBERT-V40K-klueNLI-augSTS pre-trained model (Park & Shin, 2021), which provides dense vector representations of Korean sentences. This model was specifically trained on Korean language data and fine-tuned on the KlueNLI and augmented STS datasets, making it particularly suitable for Korean semantic similarity tasks. Given two sentences, the script computes their semantic similarity through cosine similarity of their respective embeddings. Formally, for sentences S₁ and S₂ with corresponding vector embeddings e₁ and e₂, the semantic similarity (S) is calculated as:
Formula 10
Formula 10: S = (e₁ · e₂) / (||e₁|| ||e₂||)
where · denotes dot product and ||x|| represents the L2 norm. The implementation uses PyTorch’s functional operations for efficient cosine distance calculation:
similarity = F.cosine_similarity(embedding1, embedding 2, dim=0)
This measure operationalizes the abstracted semantic representation of a sentence isolated from all syntactic information. 
All metrics are applied as pairwise comparisons between original sentences and their recalled versions, resulting in similarity matrices for each measure. For the full study, which will focus on L2 English (rather than the L2 Korean of the pilot data), a version of the scripts adapted to English will be employed. The full study’s materials will include instructions on adapting the code for other languages, but it should be noted that this will require language-specific expertise in adjusting the metrics.
Pilot Data Analysis Procedure
Having run all data elicitation procedures, the pilot data analysis phase proceeded with mixed-effects modeling in order to investigate differences between L1 and L2 processing across the measured linguistic dimensions while accounting for both participant and item-level variation. Five dependent measures were analyzed: MMER representing the surface-lexis construct, DTPD representing the syntax construct, LSD representing the surface-syntax construct, HSD representing the deep-syntax construct, and STS representing the deep-meaning construct. Each measure was analyzed through two complementary approaches: (1) group comparison models examining L1/L2 differences while controlling for item length, and (2) proficiency effect models examining the relationship between L2 proficiency and performance. For each dependent measure, I fitted two linear mixed-effects models using the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015) in R:
1.	Group Comparison Model:
DV ~ group * item length + (participant) + (item)
2.	Proficiency Effect Model (L2 participants only):
DV ~ proficiency * item length + (participant) + (item)
Where DV represents each dependent measure, group is coded as 0 (L1) and 1 (L2), item length is the centered item length, and participant and item are treated as random effects, i.e., assigned random intercepts. Item length was centered to reduce multicollinearity and facilitate interpretation of main effects. Random slopes were not included due to convergence issues with the limited sample size, following the recommendations of Matuschek et al. (2017). Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and multivariate normality with Henze-Zirkler’s test. Model diagnostics included residual normality and homoscedasticity, variance inflation factors (VIF) for multicollinearity, random effects assumptions, and model convergence checks.
To address my first research question regarding the alignment of computational measures with theoretical predictions, I first calculated standardized effect sizes for group differences across measures using the method described by Westfall et al. (2014), since the measures differed in numerical scale:
Standard effect = coefficient / sqrt(total variance)
where total variance includes both random effects and residual variance. Second, I conducted pairwise comparisons between effect sizes using z-tests:
z = (effect 1 – effect 2) / sqrt(SE1^2 + SE2^2)
These comparisons were intended to quantify any systematic differences between L1 and L2 effect sizes and to establish the validity of comparisons between processing levels. To inform sample selection procedures for the proposed study, I conducted a series of post-hoc power analyses consisting of basic power calculations for unequal sample sizes using pwr.t2n.test, Monte Carlo simulations accounting for the mixed-effects structure, and power curves for various sample size combinations.
Results were visualized through several complementary approaches: standardized effect size plots showing L1-L2 differences across processing levels, interaction plots displaying group × item length relationships, proficiency gradient plots showing the relationship between L2 proficiency and each measure, and effect size comparison plots with significance indicators. All visualizations were created using ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016) with consistent formatting for comparison. 
Results
Model Diagnostics and Assumptions
Internal consistency reliability among the five measures was high (α = .85, 95% CI [.85, .86]), with good reliability maintained when examining drop-one reliability for individual measures (α range: .80-.86). The measures showed moderate inter-correlations (average r = .54, median r = .58), suggesting they capture related but distinct aspects of processing. Multivariate normality testing using Henze-Zirkler's test indicated deviation from multivariate normality (HZ = 190.37, p < .001). Univariate Anderson-Darling tests confirmed non-normality for all measures (all ps < .001). Descriptive statistics revealed varying degrees of skewness, with Linear showing the highest (skew = 2.01) and Polynomial showing the lowest (skew = 0.27). However, given the large sample size and robustness of linear mixed-effects models to non-normality, I proceeded with the analysis. Examination of Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) indicated no concerning multicollinearity, with VIF values ranging from 1.24 to 1.79 across all models. All models achieved convergence without singularity warnings, indicating stable parameter estimation.

Discussion
The pilot investigated qualitative differences between L1 and L2 mental text models through a novel multidimensional analysis approach. My results reveal systematic patterns in the relative magnitudes of L1-L2 processing differences that provide a basis for further investigation into the hierarchical organization of mental text models in L1 and L2 speakers. The pattern of effect sizes across measures aligns closely with theoretical predictions about the proportional allocation of processing resources in L1 and L2 mental text models. While L1 speakers showed better overall performance across all measures, the magnitude of these advantages varied systematically in a way that points to fundamental differences in how L1 and L2 speakers retain and organize linguistic information. This hierarchical pattern manifested with the largest L1 advantage being in semantic processing via the STS measure (standardized β = -0.790), followed by syntactic processing via the DTPD measure (standardized β = 0.771), followed by verbatim lexical processing via the MMER measure (standardized β = 0.699). Within syntactic processing, linear (standardized β = -0.571) and hierarchical (standardized β = -0.582) followed the expected order. Nevertheless, as the power analysis revealed, a larger sample size and more sensitive materials may be needed to effectively differentiate between levels, especially the linear and hierarchical syntax levels.
The pattern suggests that while L2 speakers dedicate proportionally more resources to maintaining surface-level representations, leading to smaller L1-L2 differences in these measures, L1 speakers allocate relatively more resources to deeper levels of processing, resulting in larger advantages in semantic and hierarchical measures. The polynomial syntactic measure (standardized β = 0.771), capturing overall syntactic organization, positions syntactic integration as an intermediate level in this processing hierarchy. The systematic variation in effect sizes presented here cannot be explained by simple processing limitations or capacity differences. Rather, it reflects qualitatively different patterns of stored linguistic information between L1 and L2. My findings align with predictions from both the Shallow Structure Hypothesis (SSH) and the fuzzy trace theory (FTT), while providing new evidence for how these differences manifest across multiple processing dimensions.
Proficiency Effects and the Development Trajectory
The results of the proficiency model reveal that the organization of the L2 mental text model evolves with increasing language mastery. The systematic ordering of proficiency effects (from word form: standardized β = -0.009 to semantics: standardized β = 0.020) mirrors the hierarchical pattern seen in group differences. L2 development involves a gradual shift in the proportional allocation of processing resources to deeper levels on the processing hierarchy. As proficiency increases, L2 speakers show the largest gains in semantic processing (standardized β = 0.020), with progressively smaller gains in syntax (standardized β = -0.015) and verbatim lexical retention (standardized β = -0.009).
This developmental pattern suggests that L2 speakers do not simply progress from shallow to deep processing. Instead, they gradually redistribute their processing resources across levels of representation, with proportionally greater gains in deeper processing levels. The consistency of this pattern with the group difference hierarchy supports the notion that these processing differences reflect fundamental properties of L1 and L2 mental text models.
Item Length Effects and Processing Load
The interaction between group status and item length provides additional insight into how processing resources are allocated under increasing cognitive load. L2 speakers showed greater length sensitivity in structural measures, particularly in polynomial structure (β = 0.127), suggesting that syntactic integration becomes increasingly challenging with longer utterances. However, the reduced length sensitivity in semantic processing (β = -0.011) indicates that deeper processing differences persist and remain stable even under increased load. It is expected, however, that the optimized materials, design, and sampling strategy of the full study will elicit clearer processing load effects than could be discerned from mere item length using a dataset constructed for a different purpose.
Pilot Extension: Bootstrap Simulations
The pilot study used an open data set from Isbell et al.’s (2023) study of automated speech recognition and scoring of Y. Kim et al.’s (2016) Korean version of Ortega et al.’s (2002) elicited imitation task. Findings supported the hypothesis and research questions; there was a discernible hierarchy of levels of representation from word form to syntax (polynomial>hierarchical>linear) to semantics, reflected in both the group and proficiency mixed effects models. Due, however, to the small sample of L1 participants (N = 6) in Isbell et al.’s (2023) study (as revealed by post-hoc power analyses), even though all the group and proficiency effects were significant, most of the z-test comparisons between the group effect sizes were not significant.
The power analysis led to a chosen N for the eventual full study consisting in 25 L1 participants and 40 L2 participants. The present extension to the original proposal presents data from two parametric bootstrap simulations: one based on the original N and one based on a scenario N matching the numbers chosen for the full study. These simulations were intended to serve two purposes: to test the validity of the original findings based on the less-than-ideal sampling conditions of the open data set and to evaluate the possible findings that might be generated from sampling according to the power analysis results. There are limitations to what the bootstrap simulations can reveal about the hypothetical strength of the proposed study, however, since the proposal also outlines completely new EIT items based on a critical syntactic manipulation designed to sharply increase processing load for both L1 and L2 participants—multiple center embedding. Nevertheless, it is hoped that this approach will shed light both on the strength of the pilot study and on the benefits of conducting a full study according to the designated parameters. Collectively, the three modeling approaches (pilot data, parallel simulation, and scenario simulation) are presented as evidence supporting the proposed full study, which will closely follow the methods of the pilot study, albeit with the noted amendments to sampling and materials. The hypotheses are as follows:
1. The differences between the mental text models of recently-processed input in L1 and L2 correspond to a clear hierarchy of levels of representation spanning from gist (semantic and deeper, hierarchical syntactic information) to surface form (shallower, linear syntactic and verbatim lexical information).
2. This same hierarchy of levels of representation will be represented as a function of L2 proficiency; as L2 speakers approach native-likeness, their gains in retention of information in the EIT over lower proficiency L2 speakers will approximate the proportions found in the group comparison.
3. These levels are measurable using appropriately sensitive methods for computational analysis of output in an elicited imitation task (EIT), given that the EIT is a task that uniquely provides both an index of implicit/automatized explicit knowledge (where, in the most common use case of this task, they serve as operationalizations of proficiency) and output suitable for such analysis.
Pilot Extension Analysis
To validate the findings from the pilot analysis and to assess the stability of effects under the proposed sample sizes, I conducted two types of bootstrap simulations as an extension to the original mixed-effects modeling. I first implemented a parametric bootstrap simulation of 1,000 iterations based on the parameter estimates from the original mixed-effects models. For each iteration, I simulated new random effects and residuals while maintaining the original fixed effect structure.
Second, based on the power analysis results suggesting optimal sample sizes of 25 L1 and 40 L2 participants, I conducted a scenario bootstrap simulation. This approach generated 1,000 iterations of simulated data matching the proposed sample sizes while maintaining the distributional properties of the original data. Both bootstrap procedures preserved the mixed-effects structure of the original models, with random intercepts for participants and items.
For each bootstrap iteration, I fitted the same mixed-effects models used in the pilot analysis. To assess the consistency of findings across approaches, I calculated standardized differences between each pair of approaches (original vs. parametric bootstrap, original vs. scenario bootstrap, and parametric vs. scenario bootstrap) for each processing measure. These comparisons were expressed as standardized z-statistics accounting for total variance, with corresponding p-values calculated using standard normal distribution assumptions.

This high degree of consistency across models provides support for three conclusions. First, the original findings from the pilot data appear robust to different analytical methods. Second, the effects remain stable under both parametric bootstrapping and scenario simulation with larger sample sizes. The results suggest that the processing level hierarchy observed in the pilot data is likely to be replicated in the full study with the proposed sample sizes.
The Proposed Study
The proposed study will follow the methodology outlined in this study with the sampling procedures and materials described above. The research question of the proposed study, updated in light of the manipulation present in the constructed materials, is as follows: How does processing difficulty increase across the representational hierarchy in L1 and L2 in an EIT with singly and doubly center-embedded items? The prediction is that clearer, and more significant, results will be observed with materials constructed with a manipulation that imposes a heavy processing burden in both L1 and L2. It is predicted that more balanced sampling will improve the comparison in effect sizes. Finally, reversing L1 English and L2 Korean for L1 Korean and L2 English may provide some confirmation that the methodology and the theoretical implications transcend L1 vs. L2 typological considerations.
